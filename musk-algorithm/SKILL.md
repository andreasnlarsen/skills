# Musk’s Algorithm

Improve a system by reasoning from its purpose through five ordered decisions: question requirements, delete, simplify or optimize, accelerate, then automate. Use the causes behind the sequence to decide what to change. Copying a factory tactic or completing five headings is not the outcome.

The reasons below are contributing explanations, not an exhaustive account of Musk’s motives or laws that hold in every setting. The [research reference](references/principles-and-evidence.md) distinguishes his stated reasoning, company disclosures, and this skill’s broader inferences. Read the relevant section when attribution, a difficult tradeoff, or transfer to another domain matters.

## Start with the result

Establish what must become better for whom, the required behavior, and how improvement will be observed. Separate the desired result from the current means, inherited constraints, and untested explanations. Read the actual workflow and its dependencies before recommending a change. A request to improve a process does not itself authorize changing the user’s goal or removing a required function.

Choose the depth of investigation needed for the decision. Use available evidence before asking questions, and identify an unknown when it would change the choice. Keep the analysis within the user’s authorized scope.

## Use the ordered reasoning

### 1. Question the requirements

**Principle:** Improve the question before investing in the answer. Trace a requirement to the outcome it protects, its evidence, and a person who can explain and change it.

Several reasons matter: a precise solution can serve the wrong need; a smart or senior source can discourage scrutiny; and a once-useful restriction can outlive its circumstances. An accountable person makes the reasoning inspectable where a department name or inherited document cannot.

Distinguish physical limits, externally binding obligations, user choices, and assumptions about implementation. Challenge the rationale or proposed means; do not treat uncertainty about a requirement as proof that it is false. If its original author is unavailable, seek the current accountable owner rather than stopping at historical attribution. State which evidence would support keeping, changing, or retiring it.

### 2. Try to delete the part or process

**Principle:** Test whether the required outcome can be achieved without the thing, before improving the thing.

Deletion can remove direct work, failure points, and the supporting coordination or equipment that exists because of them. It can also prevent spending effort on a needless solution. Musk’s further motivation is to counter the tendency to retain things after a memorable past omission: tolerating some restoration permits a stronger test of necessity.

Name the function the candidate serves and what else relies on it. Test omission or a smaller substitute in a way that can reveal a real loss. Restore what proves necessary. Musk’s roughly 10% restoration rule is a provocative heuristic for this bias, not a deletion quota or evidence that every system should incur that failure rate. A reversible experiment can challenge uncertainty without committing an irreversible loss.

Retention has costs too. Do not demand proof of zero possible benefit before a proportionate, reversible trial; that would preserve the conservative bias this step is meant to challenge.

### 3. Simplify or optimize what remains

**Principle:** Reduce the total burden of fulfilling a necessary function, after testing whether that function or implementation belongs in the system.

Simplification can reduce interfaces and failure opportunities, make a process easier to understand and change, and lower recurring cost. Optimization can improve a necessary tradeoff. Individually sensible choices can still form a poor system when their interactions are ignored.

Compare effects on the whole outcome: fewer visible steps or a faster component can move work elsewhere, hide complexity, or reduce reliability. Prefer the simpler implementation when it fulfills the actual requirements better. Keep complexity, redundancy, or checks that earn their cost. State what becomes easier or cheaper and which tradeoff is accepted.

### 4. Accelerate useful cycle time

**Principle:** Get the surviving useful work and its results sooner, after checking direction and method.

Speed can improve output from existing capacity, reduce delay before value arrives, and shorten the interval before evidence permits a correction. Faster feedback is a broader inference from the method; do not present every such benefit as Musk’s stated motive for step four.

Find what limits the end-to-end result, including waiting and dependencies. Distinguish elapsed time, throughput, labor time, and learning time. Accelerating a non-limiting task may change none of them materially. Check the relevant outcome and quality together. Urgency is not evidence that a delay is removable or that a person should simply work harder.

### 5. Automate a process that has earned its place

**Principle:** Scale or repeat a useful, sufficiently understood process with less recurring human effort. Avoid building machinery around assumptions that still need frequent correction.

Automation may improve repeatability, capacity, or unit economics. Deferring it can avoid embedding unnecessary steps, paying to redesign equipment, and making a wrong process harder to remove. These benefits depend on actual workload, variation, failures, and the ongoing cost of operating the automation.

Compare automation with a simpler tool, a manual method, or doing nothing further. Evaluate total work, including exceptions, supervision, recovery, maintenance, and eventual changes. AI is one possible implementation, not an exception to these questions. Manual work can teach the process; it is not a mandatory phase for every task. A small automated experiment or measuring tool can support earlier inquiry without committing the full workflow to automation.

## Apply it without turning it into ceremony

The order expresses dependencies: justify the work before refining it, and refine it before multiplying it. It is not a one-way waterfall. New evidence can send the analysis back to a requirement or deletion decision. Do not demand certainty or a complete redesign before any useful experiment.

Apply the sequence to the decision or subsystem in scope. Existing evidence can already answer earlier questions; an entire organization need not move through the stages together.

For each material recommendation, connect the proposed change to its function, the causal reason it should improve the outcome, the supporting evidence, and the next observation that could disprove it. A compact table is useful when several choices compete. Keep stated facts, assumptions, and inferences distinct.

Implement within scope when requested; otherwise make a concrete recommendation. Stop when the requested result is supported. Keeping a necessary part, restoring a deleted step, or retaining a manual process can be the right conclusion. Do not invent changes merely to reach step five.
