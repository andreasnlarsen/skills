# 80/20 Thinking Partner

Use 80/20 as a prioritization heuristic, not a fixed law or a measured ratio for the current task. Find the inputs that contribute most to the requested outcome. Preserve constraints, dependencies, and required work when reducing the remainder.

## Core discipline

- Define the outcome, time horizon, and relevant costs before ranking inputs.
- Separate observed concentration from estimates and hypotheses. Ratios such as 80/20, 95/5, and 99/1 are illustrative until measured.
- Check whether apparent low-value work enables the high-value work, reduces a material downside, or fulfills a requirement. Low direct output does not prove low total value.
- Distinguish correlation, causal contribution, and what can actually be changed. Past winners do not guarantee future returns.
- Pick the few relevant lenses below. Do not run all nine for ceremony or force a counterintuitive conclusion.
- Recommend a bounded action with its expected benefit, important tradeoff, and the smallest useful check.

## Output preference

During analysis, identify both the vital few and the apparent low-value remainder. Frame recommendations positively: what to focus on, strengthen, or make easier. This is a communication preference, not a neurological claim. State explicit stops or prohibitions when needed for safety or a clear decision.

## The 9 Foundational Frameworks

### 1. Vital Few Analysis

Measure which inputs account for disproportionate output. If data is missing, name a hypothesis and what would confirm or change it.

**Ask:** Which inputs preserve the most value after costs, dependencies, and downside are included?

Examples: customer contribution after service costs, feature use tied to customer outcomes, or profiled runtime hot spots. Measure the relevant system rather than borrowing a ratio from an anecdote.

### 2. The 20% Spike

Look for a demonstrated strength with unusually high value in this context. Build around real strengths while preserving the minimum capabilities needed for the job.

**Ask:** What is unusually effective here, and what evidence supports that judgment?

Consider enjoyment, unusual skills, peak experiences, results per unit of time, useful feedback, ease relative to peers, flow, and patterns in past achievements. Outsource only when capability, coordination cost, authority, and continuity support it.

### 3. Von Manstein Matrix

Use the historical matrix as a prompt to question needless work, not as a validated taxonomy of people. Do not classify a colleague as intelligent or stupid from effort or work style.

**Ask:** Which work can a simpler design remove while preserving the outcome?

Automation helps when its setup and maintenance cost are justified. Manual work can be the simplest sound choice for a small or changing task.

### 4. Business Genes / Mutation

Study a successful pattern and test which parts transfer to the current context. Recombination is one source of ideas, not an explanation of every successful invention.

**Ask:** What proven mechanism might fit here, and which assumptions differ?

Keep the mechanism that is supported. Change or reject inherited details that do not fit the user's constraints.

### 5. Value Innovation

Compare additions and removals together. An inexpensive improvement can matter more to the customer than an expensive convention.

**Ask:** What low-cost addition improves the outcome, and what costly element can be removed without losing required value?

Check the whole service or product. Delivery, support, accessibility, reliability, and maintenance can create value even when they are not the visible differentiator.

### 6. Time Compression

Inspect waiting, handoffs, rework, and unnecessary scope before shortening a deadline. Faster delivery can preserve or improve quality when those causes are reduced; a tighter deadline alone does not cause better work.

**Ask:** What could make delivery faster while preserving the success criteria?

Use a shorter time budget as a design exercise. Preserve necessary validation, recovery time, dependencies, and work that cannot safely be compressed.

### 7. Personal Monopoly

Look for a useful, defensible combination of strengths or a neglected need. Uniqueness has value only when someone needs the result and the position can be sustained.

**Ask:** Where can this person, team, or product serve a real need unusually well?

Balance specialization with essential breadth, changing demand, and dependence on a single market or person.

### 8. Asymmetric Bets

Compare plausible upside, downside, probability, cost, time, and reversibility. A large possible upside alone does not make a bet attractive.

**Ask:** What can be gained, what can be lost, and which uncertainty can a small authorized step resolve?

Prefer bounded experiments where useful. Check loss limits, correlated exposure, and opportunity cost before concentrating effort or resources. Watch sunk cost, fear of admitting error, and unsupported hope; difficulty alone is not a reason to quit.

### 9. Compounding the Vital Few

Look for advantages that persist and reinforce each other. Check the mechanism, reinvestment needed, saturation, changing conditions, and possible negative compounding.

**Ask:** Which useful advantage can grow through repeated effort, and what could stop it?

## Counterintuitive questions

Use these as hypotheses to test, not universal rules:

- Could less activity produce the same or better outcome?
- Would a focused strength help more than general improvement here?
- Can a simpler product or process preserve the value that matters?
- Which weakness must be repaired, and which can be supported by someone else?
- Can reduced waiting or rework improve both speed and quality?
- Would time for thought or recovery resolve the real bottleneck?
- Does this task need better execution, a better idea, or both?
- Is the chosen focus both motivating and useful to its intended audience?
